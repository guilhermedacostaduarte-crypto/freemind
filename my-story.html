How I Finally Stopped Watching Porn After 10 Years.

Posted by Willian · 7 min read

I'm 24. And for the last 10 years, I was the guy who said "I'll stop tomorrow" about a thousand times.

This is my story. I'm not writing this to sell you anything. I'm writing this because I wish someone had told me what I'm about to tell you when I was 14.

The Beginning

It started the way it starts for everyone. A friend showed me something on his phone after school.

I was 14.

I didn't think anything of it. I didn't feel anything about it. It was just there. And then it kept being there. Every day. Multiple times a day. And I didn't think that was a problem because nobody ever told me it could be one.

By 16, I was watching every night before bed. By 18, it was multiple times a day, and the content I needed to feel anything was getting more extreme. I didn't notice the escalation. You never do.

It's like the temperature rising slowly — you don't realize you're boiling until you're already cooked.

I thought I had a high sex drive. I told myself that. "I'm just a guy. This is what guys do."

But here's what I didn't understand:

What I called "high libido" wasn't desire. It was anxiety. It was boredom. It was habit.

I wasn't craving connection. I was escaping discomfort. And I had no idea there was a difference.

The Price

The first time I realized something was wrong, I was 20.

I was in my first serious relationship. And I couldn't perform.

I told myself it was nerves. Stress. Performance anxiety. I even went to a doctor. Everything was physically fine. He said "it's probably psychological" and sent me home.

It wasn't until later that I found out there's a name for it: PIED — porn-induced erectile dysfunction. I was 20 years old and my brain was so fried by pixels that my body didn't respond to a real human being.

And the worst part? I didn't connect it to porn. I kept watching. And the relationship fell apart.

After the breakup, things got worse. Not better. I had more time alone, more reasons to escape, and less reason to try.

By 22, I couldn't focus on anything for more than 20 minutes. I'd pick up my phone 100 times a day without realizing it. I'd start reading a book and quit after two pages. Music didn't hit me anymore.

I'd hang out with friends and feel like I was watching the whole thing through a window — present in body, absent in mind.

I thought I was depressed. And maybe I was. But the depression wasn't coming from nowhere. It was coming from the fact that I had numbed my own brain to the point where nothing felt real anymore.

I didn't enjoy food. I didn't enjoy conversations. I didn't enjoy anything unless it was fast, loud, and on a screen. And even that was starting to feel like nothing.

The Night Everything Changed

It was a Tuesday. 2:47 AM.

I was sitting on my bed after another session I didn't enjoy, didn't want, and couldn't explain. The screen was off. The room was quiet. And I realized I couldn't remember the last time I'd felt genuinely excited about anything.

Not a girl. Not a goal. Not a sunrise. Nothing.

I wasn't sad. I wasn't angry. I was just flat.

And that flatness — that absence of feeling — scared me more than anything ever had. Because I realized: I'm 23 and I feel like I'm 80 inside.

That night I didn't promise to quit. I'd done that a hundred times.

Instead, I did something I'd never done: I started reading about what was actually happening to my brain. Not self-help blogs. Not motivational videos. The actual neuroscience.

And what I found was like someone turning on a light in a dark room I'd been stumbling around in for a decade.

The Discovery

I found out that dopamine isn't the pleasure chemical. It's the wanting chemical. It doesn't make you feel good. It makes you crave.

And I had been trapped in the craving loop for 10 years — chasing things that didn't even give me pleasure anymore.

I found out that the brain physically changes in response to chronic porn use. Dopamine receptors downregulate — they literally reduce in number to protect the brain from overstimulation.

The numbness I felt wasn't a character flaw. It was a biological adaptation. My brain had turned down the volume to survive the noise.

I found out about something called the Coolidge Effect — how unlimited novelty destroys your ability to appreciate one person, one thing, one moment. Because your brain has been trained to always want the next thing. Always. The next video. The next image. The next tab.

And that hunger follows you into real life, where there are no tabs and no "next" buttons.

But most importantly: I found out that it's reversible.

The brain can heal. It's called neuroplasticity. But it doesn't happen by trying harder. It happens by giving the brain the right conditions to reset.

The Rebuild

I'm not going to walk you through every technique I used. This isn't a tutorial.

But I'll tell you the things that actually mattered.

I stopped relying on willpower. Willpower is a muscle that's already exhausted by 8 PM — which is exactly when most relapses happen. Instead, I changed my environment.

Phone in grayscale. DNS blocker on my router. Phone charging in the kitchen, not the bedroom.

These weren't "tips." They were barriers that made the wrong thing harder and the right thing easier.

I learned to ride out urges instead of fighting them. An urge lasts about 15-20 minutes. If you can sit with it — not suppress it, not fight it, just watch it — it passes. Every time.

I set a timer on my phone and waited. Some nights I set the timer 5 times. But each wave I survived made the next one smaller.

The hardest part was what they call the flatline. Around week 2, everything went dead. No libido. No motivation. Food tasted like cardboard. I was convinced I'd broken myself permanently.

But the science said this was the brain healing — like a cast on a broken bone. So I kept going.

Lifted weights when I had zero motivation. Forced myself to be around people when I felt numb.

And around day 19, something shifted.

I woke up and the sunlight through my window looked... warm. My coffee tasted like coffee. A song I'd heard a hundred times hit me in the chest.

I felt something. Real, genuine, unmedicated feeling.

I sat on my bed and cried. Not from sadness. From relief.

Where I Am Now

I'm 7 months clean now.

I'm not going to pretend my life is perfect. But I can focus for hours without reaching for my phone. I can be in a room with people and actually be there. I enjoy a walk, a meal, a conversation — things that used to feel unbearable.

I'm not "fighting every day." The system I built means I rarely have to fight at all.

I'm not a doctor. I'm not a therapist. I'm not selling anything. I'm just a 24-year-old who lost 10 years to something nobody ever warned him about, and who finally found a way out.

If you're where I was — sitting in the dark at 2 AM, feeling nothing, wondering if it's too late — it's not.

Your brain heals. It comes back. You just have to give it a chance.
